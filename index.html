Scientists are making progress in the fight against malaria, with vaccine research offering new hope for protecting millions of people worldwide. As countries work to expand access to vaccination and improve prevention, researchers continue to explore ways to reduce infections and save lives. Science & Health · Feature

Malaria Vaccine Research: What You Need to Know

A closer look at the research, the public-health challenge, and what happens next.

By Monty Hamon · Updated 26 October 2026
Image caption: UNICEF Ethiopia / Demissew Bizuwerk.

Overview Malaria remains a major global health challenge, but advances in vaccine development are creating new opportunities to protect vulnerable communities. Researchers and public health organisations are working to expand access to malaria vaccines, particularly in regions where the disease causes the greatest burden of illness. These efforts could help reduce severe cases and deaths, especially among young children, while supporting existing prevention methods such as mosquito nets and other control measures.

What researchers are investigating

Griffith University researchers are developing a vaccine called PlasProtecT. Unlike vaccines that mainly target an earlier stage of the malaria parasite, this one is designed to target the parasite when it is in the blood. The vaccine contains killed malaria parasites, along with other ingredients that help the immune system respond. The researchers reported that early tests in animals showed protection against malaria parasites. These are pre-clinical results, which means animal and laboratory findings alone cannot prove that the vaccine will work safely in people. Earlier versions of the vaccine have also been studied in small human studies, but that does not prove that the newer freeze-dried version will work the same way.

A useful feature of PlasProtecT is that it can be frozen or freeze-dried into a powder without losing its effect in the studies reported by Griffith. Freeze-drying removes water so a product can be stored as a powder. This could make a vaccine easier to transport to places where reliable refrigeration is difficult. However, scientists still need to test how safe and effective it is in people and how well it can be stored over time.

“You could keep it on the shelf for a couple of months. Or, if you had to keep it in the fridge and your fridge went off for a week, you wouldn’t have to throw it all out.”
— Associate Professor Danielle Stanisic, Griffith University

How the vaccine works

Malaria is caused by tiny parasites called Plasmodium. They usually enter a person when an infected female Anopheles mosquito bites them. The parasites travel through the body and can infect red blood cells, causing symptoms such as fever, chills and headaches. A vaccine prepares the immune system to recognise parts of a germ or parasite, so the body can respond more effectively later. PlasProtecT uses killed parasites from the blood stage to show the immune system many parasite proteins. The aim is to help immune cells called T cells recognise and attack infected cells. This is a different approach from the RTS,S and R21 vaccines, which the World Health Organization recommends for children in areas where malaria is common.

Why it matters

Malaria is still a major health problem. The World Health Organization estimated that there were 282 million malaria cases and 610,000 malaria deaths worldwide in 2024. Children under five are especially at risk. Existing vaccines, mosquito nets treated with insecticide, mosquito control, testing and early treatment can all help reduce the harm caused by malaria.

There is evidence that vaccination can make a difference. In pilot programmes in Ghana, Kenya and Malawi, the RTS,S vaccine was given to children through routine vaccination services. The World Health Organization reported a 13% reduction in deaths from all causes among children eligible for the vaccine, as well as fewer hospitalisations for severe malaria. These results are about the RTS,S vaccine in those countries, not the experimental PlasProtecT vaccine or Australia. They show why researchers are continuing to develop and improve malaria vaccines.

What does this mean for Australia?

Australia was declared free of endemic malaria in 1981, but people still arrive with malaria after being infected overseas. Rare local transmission has also happened in parts of northern Australia, including the Torres Strait, where suitable mosquitoes live. This means malaria vaccines are mainly important for protecting people in countries where malaria spreads regularly, and for research that could help those communities. For Australians travelling to places where malaria is common, medical travel advice, mosquito bite prevention and any recommended medicines are still important. The PlasProtecT research does not mean a new vaccine is already available for travellers.

What happens next?

Griffith’s 2025 Malaria Vaccine Project newsletter said the freeze-dried version was being prepared for a Phase I clinical trial in Melbourne. A Phase I trial mainly checks safety and how the body responds to the vaccine. I could not confirm published results from that specific trial in the sources available for this article. Researchers will also need to check how long protection lasts and how well it works against different parasite strains. Even if results are promising, it can take more studies before a vaccine is approved for general use.

Checking the sources

Griffith University’s research announcement is useful because it comes from the university whose scientists developed PlasProtecT and describes the study and the researchers’ explanation. However, it is a university news story, so it mainly reports the team’s own work and should not be the only source used to judge whether the vaccine works in people.

The World Health Organization’s malaria vaccine information is reliable for public-health guidance because WHO reviews evidence and gives recommendations for countries. It is useful for understanding the results of RTS,S and R21 programmes, but those results should not be treated as proof that PlasProtecT has the same effect.
